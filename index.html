01

What PBN actually is

A US dental practice is a small business. One to four dentists, five to twenty-five staff, and one system of record called the practice management system (PMS) that owns the schedule, the chart, the ledger and the insurance claims. Dentrix, Eaglesoft and Open Dental are the big three.

Almost every other dental software company is a satellite orbiting that PMS. It reads data out, does something the PMS does badly or not at all, and writes results back. Practice by Numbers is one of those satellites, and a broad one: it layers analytics, patient communication, a phone system, payments, digital forms and a set of AI agents on top of a database it does not own.

That one structural fact explains most of what follows. PBN’s correctness depends on a third-party schema it cannot control, frequently running on a Windows box in the back office of a strip-mall dental practice. Its moat is the quality of that sync layer. Its support burden lives there too.

The one-sentence version

PBN replaces the six-to-eight separate subscriptions a typical dental practice pays for — reminders, forms, payments, phones, reviews, analytics — with one platform that syncs bidirectionally with the practice’s existing PMS, priced per location from $249 a month.

Who buys it

PBN maintains seven persona pages, and the buyer differs meaningfully by segment. The office manager is the day-to-day power user and often the internal champion. The dentist-owner signs. For multi-location groups and DSOs, a central operations or finance committee decides, and the sale becomes a procurement exercise with a security review attached. Dental consultants and marketing agencies are a third motion: they resell visibility to their own clients, and PBN pays them up to 8.5% recurring revenue share through a referral partner program.

Note one gap against the common assumption: there is no CPA persona page. CPAs appear only as a referral partner type and in a single testimonial.

Company, founders, scale

Three co-founders, and the mix is the story PBN tells about itself constantly:

  • Dr. Aditi Agarwal, DMD — co-founder, a practicing dentist who graduated Boston University in 2003 and opened her first practice in 2010. She also hosts the company podcast.
  • Rohit Garg — CEO and co-founder, joined the effort in 2011. Crunchbase lists his title as President, which is a small inconsistency worth not tripping over.
  • Chris Lau, PhD — CTO and co-founder.

The founding line is “running a dental practice shouldn’t be harder than treating patients,” and the recurring proof point on nearly every page is built by dentists who understand real workflows. That clinician credibility is the company’s primary differentiator against venture-funded competitors, and it is genuinely earned.

Shape of the team

Fully remote, distributed across the US, UK, India, Bangladesh and Vietnam. The public team page lists roughly 81 people by first name only. The functional mix is revealing: about 25 in customer experience, support and success, about 18 in engineering, 11 in marketing, 9 in sales. Roughly a third of the company is customer-facing.

That matters because responsive support is the single most-praised attribute across PBN’s ~50 testimonials, and slow support is the criticism PBN levels at all nine of its named competitors. The headcount backs the claim, and a 4.9/5 across 104 G2 reviews corroborates it from a third direction. Of everything PBN asserts, this is the best-supported claim.

Headcount is unreconciled

The About page says “100+ global employees,” Crunchbase says 51–100, Growjo estimates 73, and the team page lists ~81. Founding year is similarly muddled: the About page says 2015 while its own milestone timeline starts in 2010. Probably incorporation versus origin, but do not quote either figure as settled.

Security posture, and the gap in it

PBN claims TLS 1.2+ in transit, AES-256 at rest, US-based cloud hosting with logical per-practice tenant separation, role-based access control, enforceable MFA, access logging against permissions, a formal incident response procedure with HIPAA breach notification, background-checked staff, and business associate agreements on request.

SOC 2 is not claimed anywhere on the security page. The footer links a Drata trust center, which is a compliance-automation vendor, so a SOC 2 program is plausible — but Type 1 versus Type 2, auditor and audit date are all unknown. Penetration testing, data retention specifics and GDPR posture are also absent. For anyone selling into healthcare or courting DSOs, that missing attestation is the first thing procurement asks for.

03

The Frankenstack thesis

PBN’s entire market position — homepage, all seven persona pages, all nine comparison pages, and its strongest case study — is one argument. You are paying six vendors for six logins, your front desk retypes the same data into all of them, and none of them tells you whether any of it worked. We replace all six.

Their best proof asset is Atlanta Dental Center, a two-doctor, ten-operatory practice on on-premise Dentrix that consolidated six subscriptions into PBN:

MeasureResultWhat it was before
Monthly tech spend−$475Six separate subscriptions
Total software spend−33%—
Staff time reclaimed+3 hrs/wkDuplicate data entry across tools
Patient confirmation rate+27%Separate reminder vendor
Treatment acceptance+17%No unscheduled-treatment visibility

The owner’s quote is the thesis stated plainly: “We had all these different technology pieces… each had a different vendor, and each system required its own login, workflow, and training.”

Reinforcing it structurally: PBN bills per location, not per provider or per seat. The terms of use define a location as a licensed office. That is friendlier than per-provider pricing for a multi-dentist single office, and it is a real competitive fact rather than marketing.

Handle this stat carefully

PBN’s most-repeated headline is a 566% increase in same-day treatment acceptance at Midtown Dental Studio. No baseline is published. A 566% lift implies a very low starting number, so treat it as directionally impressive and statistically soft. The same case’s collections figure (+36%) and visit growth (+17%) are more defensible.

05

Business Analytics

This is where PBN started and still the centre of gravity. The positioning line is “All Your Practice Metrics, in One Simple View.”

Practice IQThe core dashboard

Customisable dashboards over 600+ KPIs, with peer benchmarking against national practices (“Track and Compare”), a Revenue Opportunities view for hidden revenue queries, and custom tags for profitability analysis. Single sign-on across an organisation.

Two AI layers ride on top: PiQ Insights for automated KPI summaries and trend analysis, and PiQ Assistant for natural-language queries that return a visualisation.

Revenue IQThe action layer

Where analytics becomes a worklist. Patient Finder offers 40+ filters plus AI search to surface unscheduled treatment plans, patients with remaining insurance benefits, inactive patients and overdue recalls. Saved searches build a reusable filter library.

Smart Tasking then auto-assigns follow-ups with overdue/due-today/upcoming status, tracks call attempts and outcomes, and can launch a campaign against the segment. Claimed reappointment improvement of 30–50%.

Enterprise DashboardMulti-location roll-up

The DSO surface. Single sign-on across locations, drill-down from group to office to provider, cross-location comparison on production, collections, retention, treatment acceptance, hygiene productivity and scheduling efficiency. Pitched for 5 to 500+ locations.

Daily HuddleThe ten-minute morning meeting

Not a dashboard so much as a scripted standup. Yesterday’s numbers, scheduled production against today’s goal with the specific gap-closing opportunities, new-patient alerts, patients arriving with outstanding balances, pending treatment plans with talking points, and unscheduled family members accompanying a patient.

This is the module practices talk about most warmly in testimonials, and it is worth understanding why: it converts analytics into a daily ritual the team performs, rather than a report nobody opens.

Internal inconsistency

The KPI count is stated as 600+ on Business Analytics, Practice IQ and the Dental Intel comparison page, but 400+ on the Enterprise Dashboard page. Similarly the homepage’s five headline stats (375+ hours saved, 32% fewer claim denials, 40% more reviews, 48+ chairs filled, 99.99% uptime) carry no footnote or methodology anywhere on the site and trace to no published case study.

06

PRM and Voice

Patient Relationship Management is the module set that fights the two things that cost a dental practice most: empty chairs and patients who drift away.

What is actually in it

  • Patient Reminders — text, email and voice, with configurable timing and tone, and family messaging that sends one notification to the primary contact when several family members have same-day appointments. Responses update the PMS in real time.
  • Two-way texting — sent from the practice’s existing phone line, with the full conversation stored in the PMS as patient notes.
  • Online booking — reads live schedule availability and writes appointments back either automatically or on approval. Configurable by time slot, operatory, provider or appointment type, with Google Reserve integration so patients can book from Search and Maps, and patient blocking for high-risk cases or specific insurance types.
  • Review management — automated post-visit requests to Google, Yelp and Facebook, with smart exclusions that filter by treatment type, custom tag or PMS note so you do not ask the patient who just had a bad extraction.
  • Patient portal — balances, stored cards, appointments, forms, treatment plans and consent signing, custom-branded.
  • Campaign tiers — Essential (four pre-built workflows: appointment reminder, hygiene pre-appointment, recall follow-up, cancelled-appointment follow-up), Custom (200+ templates), and Advanced (behaviour-triggered).

The follow-up escalation pattern

Worth knowing in detail, because it is a good piece of product design. A follow-up campaign sends on the patient’s preferred channel. If there is no response it tries alternate channels. If those fail, it creates a staff task for a human phone call. Six campaign types run this way: recall (typically six-month, resets on cancellation), reactivation (inactive 365+ days), unscheduled treatment, cancelled appointment, failed appointment, and post-op instructions.

PbN Voice

Cloud VoIP with touch-screen handsets, a soft dialer, and unlimited calling. The features that matter are the PMS-integrated ones: call pop-ups showing patient details before you answer, parked calls displayed with patient names, voicemail-to-text transcribed and logged into the PMS, virtual fax with no hardware, and call rescue texts that turn a missed call into a booked appointment. Smart routing works by time, department or location. An AI add-on layers call summaries written back to the PMS, call scoring for staff coaching, and AI-drafted follow-ups.

PBN will also buy you out of your existing phone contract, which tells you how strategically important owning the line is to them.

07

Operational Efficiency

The suite aimed squarely at front-desk labour. Claimed effect: 20–40 hours saved weekly per front-desk team, 25–30% faster check-in-to-payment cycle, 40% fewer manual errors.

Insurance VerificationThe highest-ROI module

Verification triggered automatically by practice-defined rules, plus bulk verification of an entire day’s schedule and guest verification for prospective patients. Completed verifications save as PDFs into the PMS document center. AI-generated summaries highlight coverage limits, frequencies, deductibles and financial details.

The report returns coverage start and end dates, alerts for terminated or inactive plans, deductible met and remaining, and in-network co-pay specifics. This module has the widest PMS support on the platform — 19 systems — because it is read-only and therefore easier to integrate broadly.

Patient FlowThe operatory board

A live status dashboard tracking checked-in, seated and check-out, with drag-and-drop between statuses and rooms. A ready-to-seat state separates arrived from clinically ready. Digital routing slips carry patient details, alerts, clinical notes, service codes and checkout tasks. Unlimited custom alerts (“Need Hygiene Check”, “10 Mins Behind”), instant provider summoning to a room, and status changes written back to the PMS.

Advanced Task ManagementThe accountability layer

Bulk task creation from the Huddle interface across one or many patients, custom task types grouped as general, patient care, appointments, follow-ups and communications, with assignment, due dates, and required outcome selection plus notes on completion.

Smart FormsIntake without paper

Digital forms delivered by SMS or email before the appointment, built in a drag-and-drop builder with conditional logic, bundled into a single assignment link, in English and Spanish, with an embedded payment block and authentication by last name plus date of birth or a one-time password.

Web Kiosk runs in a browser on an iPad or desktop, locked to the check-in app via Guided Access, with PIN two-factor across unlimited registered devices, QR handoff to the patient’s own phone, ID card and insurance capture, patient photo upload, and live treatment plan retrieval from the PMS. Ops AI summarises submitted forms and flags allergies and medications.

PbN Grow AppMobile

iOS and Android. Schedule, two-way texting, mobile Practice IQ and Revenue IQ dashboards, payment requests, patient search, VoIP calling on the office line (requires PbN Voice), review prompts, booking and team chat. Face ID / Touch ID, role-based access, and no sensitive patient data stored on device.

08

PbN AI

Four products. Understand up front that in September 2026 an AI receptionist, call analytics and automated insurance verification are table stakes across this entire category, not differentiators. Every named competitor ships all three. Differentiation has moved to depth of PMS writeback.

AI ReceptionistVoice agent

Handles after-hours, lunch-break and overflow calls. Books directly into the PMS against real-time availability, handles cancellations and confirmations, answers balance inquiries with identity verification, triages to the right provider and operatory, explains cancellation policies, and hands off to human staff with call context. Runs on a customisable knowledge base, single login, toggleable on and off.

The pitch stat: roughly 35% of calls go unanswered during business hours, and a third of those are new patients worth $10,000+ in lifetime value.

Call AIConversation intelligence

Transcription with topic recognition, sentiment detection, keyword highlighting for insurance, emergencies, reschedules and first-time callers, caller-type identification, and AI summaries extracting purpose, outcome, action items and unresolved issues. The dashboard reports answer rates, missed calls, negative-sentiment percentage, new-patient conversion and quantified lost opportunity.

Requires PbN Voice. Sold as an add-on. Advanced features (call scoring, agent performance, coaching, instant negative-call alerts) sit in the higher tier, not base Call AI.

Ops AIAdmin automation

Six components, and this is the most immediately useful set: PiQ Assistant and RiQ Assistant for natural-language analytics, Form Summary which flags medication allergies out of intake forms, Message Creation for SMS and email drafts, Review Response with adjustable tone, and Insurance Summary which extracts coverage breakdowns from payer PDFs.

IntelliSuiteThe bundle

Ops AI plus Call AI Plus in one engine. Adds advanced call scoring, predictive performance forecasting, review categorisation by sentiment, outcome tracking, multi-language translation of emails, texts, transcripts and notes, and voice fingerprinting for caller verification.

Where the real difficulty is

The hard engineering in an AI receptionist is not the voice model. It is the write. Booking an appointment means reading real operatory availability, provider schedules, appointment-type durations and block-scheduling rules out of the PMS, then writing an appointment back correctly. That is a PMS integration problem, and it is exactly where a company with a mature sync layer beats a voice-AI startup. Note too that TCPA applies to AI-initiated outbound calls, and the model provider needs a business associate agreement.

Payments and Marketing

PbN Payments

Embedded payments are the highest-margin attach product in this category, which is why every competitor sells one. PBN’s version claims 500+ practices on its POS and 100,000+ transactions monthly, with a 30% reduction in outstanding patient balances and A/R cycles 15 days faster.

The mechanics: text-to-pay links with the statement or treatment plan attached; a public payment portal a patient can use with only a name and date of birth, no login; card-present via Castles S1F2 or Verifone P400 Plus terminals; and A/R automation that segments patients by claim status, balance size or tag and runs up to ten automated follow-ups each. Every payment posts to the PMS ledger automatically, with failed-sync alerts to the action center.

Payment Plans is the in-house financing alternative to third-party lenders: weekly, bi-weekly or monthly schedules, configurable down payments and discounts, automatic card charging with smart retry (five attempts over one week), aging triggers at 30/60/90+ days, and plan pausing up to 30 days.

ZeroPay is worth understanding as a compliance product, not just a pricing one. It automatically excludes debit, prepaid and certain business cards from surcharging, handles regional rules, and shows the surcharge as a separate receipt line. Surcharging is permitted in 46 states and prohibited or restricted in California, Connecticut, Maine and Massachusetts. That is card-network rules and state law, not PCI.

Dental Marketing Suite

The loop this suite closes is the one every dental marketing agency struggles to prove: which ad produced which patient, and how much dentistry did that patient actually buy.

  • Marketing IQ / Marketing ROI — lead source identification across Facebook, Google, Yelp, direct and referral; dynamic number insertion giving each campaign its own tracking number; attribution from first contact through treatment completion and payment. Reports cost per acquisition by source, patient lifetime value, and production by lead source rather than just lead count. Integrates Google Ads, Google Analytics, Google Tag Manager and QuickBooks — the QuickBooks link is what lets it compute real ROI against actual spend.
  • Call tracking — Smart Caller ID showing patient name, treatment plan, outstanding balance and notes; “PhoneIQ” identifying likely new-patient calls. Works with an existing landline or VoIP without changing numbers, which makes it the low-friction entry point.
  • Webchat — asynchronous, so the conversation survives the visitor closing the tab and continues by text. Multi-location routing, group chat, in-conversation booking.
  • Custom Campaigns — 200+ email and SMS templates with targeting by visit history, treatment need and engagement.
TCPA exposure lives here

Appointment reminders fall under the healthcare exemption and need only prior express consent. Whitening promotions, membership pitches and “use your benefits before year-end” campaigns are marketing and need prior express written consent. That last one is a genuinely contested gray area and practices love sending it. Section 16 has the detail.

The integration layer

If you learn one thing deeply, learn this. It is the technical heart of the company and the least documented part of the public site — almost everything below comes from the help center rather than marketing pages.

The supported systems, which differ by module

PBN advertises six PMS platforms in nearly every page footer: Dentrix, Dentrix Ascend, Practice-Web, Eaglesoft, Open Dental, Curve Dental. But the real support matrix varies by module, and this is a nuance to hold:

ModuleSystems supportedWhy the difference
Insurance Verification19Read-only; adds OrthoTrac, SoftDent, Dolphin, Tracker, Fuse, ABELDent, DentiMax, PracticeWorks, Sensei Cloud and others
Standard footer claim6The marketed set
Online booking5Drops Curve Dental; needs schedule writeback
Operational Efficiency4Dentrix, Dentrix Ascend, Eaglesoft, Open Dental only
Payments (two-way)4 + versionsDentrix G7.2+, Open Dental 20+, Dentrix Ascend, Eaglesoft 17+ — ledger writeback is the deepest integration

The pattern is consistent: the deeper the write, the narrower the support. Any “works with your PMS” claim needs qualifying per module.

How the sync actually works

It is primarily a read integration with configurable writeback. PBN’s own words for Dentrix Ascend: “the system functions primarily as a data-reading integration, though write-back behavior is fully configurable.” Practices control what syncs back under Practice Settings › PMS Write-Back Configuration.

  • Initial sync takes 1–3 hours depending on history volume. The platform is usable during it, but reports show incomplete data.
  • Ongoing sync is incremental — only modified or new records move. Seven configurable intervals from 2 to 60 minutes, 15 the default. PBN recommends 30–60 min for practices under 50 daily appointments, 10–15 for 50–150, and 5–10 above 150.
  • Writeback is faster and separate: appointment status every 15 seconds, new patients every 30, appointment changes every 60.
  • What gets written back: SMS conversations as patient notes, email history, phone call logs typed as incoming/outgoing/missed including transcripts and summaries, appointment status changes, uploaded documents, submitted forms, new patient records and appointments, task notes, insurance verification notes, and payments to the ledger. Note type, document type, appointment status mapping and payment type mapping are all configurable.
  • Per-PMS quirks: Open Dental requires “Show Automated Commlogs” enabled for SMS logs. Eaglesoft and Dentrix require provider ID or code configuration.
  • Cloud versus on-premise: Dentrix Ascend connects by API with a client ID and secret, stored encrypted over TLS. Server-based systems depend on the practice’s own machine — the help center tells practices to “ensure your server is powered on and connected to the internet” and to have someone with server access available during a support call.
  • Multi-location: each Dentrix Ascend location needs a separate PBN practice account with its own connection. The Enterprise Dashboard unifies them under single sign-on.
  • Manual override: a Force Incremental Sync button triggers an immediate cycle. A sync-issue banner appears on the dashboard when the connection breaks.
The undocumented part

PBN never names an on-premise agent or connector product publicly. No system requirements, no ports, no firewall rules, no architecture diagram. The dependency is only inferable from troubleshooting language. There is a downloadable “PbN Printer” local install used to push statements and treatment plans into PBN. If you need the real architecture, ask — and read section 12 for how the comparable integrations work at Open Dental and Henry Schein One, which are documented.

You cannot understand PBN without understanding what it plugs into. Roughly half the installed base is still on-premise Windows software, which is the single fact that shapes the engineering of this whole category.

Open Dental is the one to study, because it is the only major system with public, well-designed integration docs. Resources map roughly one-to-one to database tables. Auth carries two keys, a developer key and a per-practice customer key, as Authorization: ODFHIR {dev}/{customer}. The eConnector is a Windows service on the practice server that holds the database credentials, reports heartbeats, and brokers remote API traffic — and if the machine sleeps, everything stops. Two sharp edges worth internalising: normal user permissions log API actions but do not restrict them, and date restrictions the desktop app enforces on staff do not apply to the API. Least privilege is your problem, not the platform’s. Reading those docs is the fastest way to understand the shape of dental data.

Do not quote PMS market share

There is no reliable public dataset. Vendor self-reported installed bases are non-comparable, and the widely circulated 6sense percentages come from web-technology fingerprinting over a ~6,600-company sample against a market of 120,000+ practice locations — it systematically undercounts on-premise desktop software and mixes PMS with engagement products. The defensible qualitative read: Dentrix and Eaglesoft together hold the largest general-dentistry share, Open Dental is fastest-growing among independents and hugely over-represented among integrators, and cloud is where net-new and DSO growth goes but remains a minority of the installed base.

The aggregators, who are also competitors

Sikka Software claims 400+ PMS integrations and 188M+ patients, sells a unified API to developers via an on-premise extraction agent — and has since moved up-stack into its own end product. NexHealth sells the Synchronizer universal API with bearer auth, webhooks, a sandbox and an OpenAPI spec, while also competing as a patient-experience product. Both illustrate the platform risk in this market: Open Dental publicly characterises NexHealth as an unauthorised third party. If you build on an aggregator, your PMS access is only as durable as their relationship with the PMS vendor.

And the deepest structural tension: Henry Schein One is simultaneously PBN’s most important data supplier and a competitor across nearly every module — it owns Dentrix, Dentrix Ascend, Jarvis Analytics, Sesame and Lighthouse 360.

13

DSOs

A Dental Service Organization contracts with dental practices to provide everything non-clinical: HR, payroll, marketing, procurement, IT, revenue cycle, real estate, compliance. The clinical entity stays dentist-owned and keeps clinical decision-making.

The split exists because most states have corporate practice of dentistry laws barring non-dentists from owning a practice. The structure is a dentist-owned professional corporation signing a long-term management services agreement with the DSO, which takes a management fee. That is the legal chassis that lets private equity capitalise dentistry.

Scale, from the ADA Health Policy Institute and the ADSO: 16% of US dentists were DSO-affiliated as of 2024. Private-practice ownership fell from 84.7% of dentists in 2005 to 72.5% in 2023. ADSO members alone cover 8,500+ practices and roughly $18B in revenue. New dentists graduate with heavy debt and are reluctant to add acquisition debt; retiring dentists need an exit and DSOs are the buyer of record.

KPIs that matter

Learn these definitions properly. Two analytics products will report different numbers for the same practice, almost always because of adjustment timing, provider attribution, or a different active-patient window — and that disagreement is the most common “your data is wrong” support ticket in this category.

MetricDefinitionThe trap
ProductionDollar value of dentistry performed at full fee in a periodGross vs net (net subtracts writeoffs). Always state which
CollectionsCash actually received in the periodNot tied to the same procedures as the period’s production
Collection ratioCollections ÷ productionAgainst gross production it looks terrible in a PPO-heavy practice. Target ~98% against net, rolling 12 months
Writeoff vs adjustmentWriteoff is the contractual PPO discount; adjustment is discretionary (courtesy, discount, bad debt)Conflating them is a classic bug. Writeoffs measure contract mix, adjustments measure discipline

Revenue cycle

The flow: verify eligibility → present treatment with an out-of-pocket estimate → perform the procedure → code it in CDT → submit an electronic claim with attachments through a clearinghouse → payer adjudicates → ERA returns → post the payment, post the contractual writeoff, bill the patient the remainder → work the denials.

Why insurance verification is a product category

Eligibility rides the X12 270 inquiry and 271 response pair. The catch, and the entire reason this is a business: the dental 271 is thin. It reliably returns active or inactive, but frequently omits the frequency limitations, downgrade clauses and category percentages that actually determine what the practice gets paid. So verification products stack portal automation, document parsing and payer-specific rules on top of the 270/271. That is a good fit for LLMs, it is high-volume, it is expensive to do manually at ten to fifteen minutes per patient by phone, and it is measurable. Hence every vendor in this category selling it.

An EOB is the human-readable explanation of how a claim was adjudicated. An ERA is the 835 equivalent. Auto-posting an ERA into the ledger — correctly splitting insurance payment, contractual writeoff and patient responsibility per line — is one of the highest-value automations in dental, and where AI payment-posting products are competing.

CDT codes

The Code on Dental Procedures and Nomenclature, maintained by the ADA and mandated under HIPAA for dental procedures in standard electronic transactions. Format is D plus four digits, in series: D0100–D0999 diagnostic, D1000–D1999 preventive, D2000–D2999 restorative, D3000–D3999 endodontics, D4000–D4999 periodontics, D5000–D5899 removable prosthodontics, D6000–D6199 implants, D6200–D6999 fixed prosthodontics, D7000–D7999 oral surgery, D8000–D8999 orthodontics, D9000–D9999 adjunctive.

Two engineering consequences. The code set changes every January 1 — new, deleted and revised codes — so fee schedules, mappings and analytics groupings need an annual migration path. And CDT is ADA-copyrighted and licensed; redistributing the full code set with descriptors requires a licence.

The dental-specific twist

A dental claim line often carries tooth number and surface, and is invalid without them. Universal numbering is 1–32 for permanent teeth, A–T for primary, plus supernumeraries and the international FDI and Palmer notations. That is a real data-modelling concern.

Attachments are also distinctly dental: payers routinely require X-rays, perio charts and narratives before paying crowns, endo, perio surgery or implants. Getting an image out of the imaging system, onto the right claim, in the payer’s accepted format is its own product category — Vyne Dental processes 40M+ attachments a year, and DentalXChange handles ~300M claims annually across ~1,400 payer connections.

Plan mechanics that shape every dental product

  • ~82% of dental policies are PPOs (NADP). The dentist accepts a reduced allowed fee, typically 20–40% off their posted UCR fee, and may not balance-bill the patient the difference. That gap is the mandatory contractual writeoff.
  • Annual maximums are mostly under $1,500, a figure essentially unchanged for decades. This single fact is why “use your benefits before year-end” campaigns exist in every patient-engagement product and why they work.
  • Coverage tiers run roughly preventive 100%, basic 80%, major 50%, with $50–$100 deductibles and waiting periods on individual policies.
  • Frequency limits (two cleanings a year, bitewings every 12 months, a crown every five to seven years per tooth), downgrade clauses (payer pays amalgam, you placed composite) and coordination of benefits for dual coverage are the three reasons an “estimated patient portion” is so often wrong.

HIPAA, TCPA, PCI

HIPAA — you are the business associate

The dental practice is the covered entity. A vendor like PBN is a business associate: a person or entity performing functions on behalf of a covered entity involving creating, receiving, maintaining or transmitting protected health information. HHS explicitly names cloud service providers, IT vendors, billing services and AI chatbots on patient portals as examples.

In dental software, essentially everything you touch is PHI. Note especially that appointment metadata alone is PHI — “Jane Smith, 2pm, root canal” is a disclosure. And your subcontractors are business associates too, which means your cloud host, SMS gateway, transcription vendor and LLM provider all need to be under BAA.

Business associates bear direct liability. OCR can fine you independently; the relationship with the covered entity is not a shield.

Minimum necessary requires limiting use, disclosure and requests to what is needed for the purpose — internally, role-based access policies identifying which employees need which data. Note the sharp edge from section 12: Open Dental’s API grants coarse permissions and bypasses the date restrictions the desktop app enforces, so minimum necessary is your implementation problem, not something the API enforces for you.

Architecturally that means encryption in transit and at rest, RBAC and per-tenant isolation, immutable audit logging of every PHI access (practices get audited and will ask you for it), a 60-day breach notification clock, no PHI in logs or error trackers or analytics pipelines, and careful handling of PHI in LLM prompts. SOC 2 Type II has become the de facto commercial requirement layered on top.

TCPA — the one most likely to bite

Implemented in 47 CFR § 64.1200. It carries a private right of action with statutory damages of $500–$1,500 per message and class actions are routine.

  • Prior express written consent is required for advertising and telemarketing by automated system or prerecorded voice, authorising messages to a specified number, disclosing that consent is not a condition of purchase.
  • Prior express consent (oral suffices) covers the healthcare exemption: appointment confirmations, wellness checkups, lab results and post-discharge follow-up, provided the messages comply with HIPAA privacy rules and stay within frequency caps of one per day and three per week per patient.
  • Revocation by any reasonable method, expressly including STOP, QUIT, END, REVOKE, OPT OUT, CANCEL and UNSUBSCRIBE, honoured within ten business days.

What that means in a product: keep per-patient, per-channel consent records with provenance and timestamp, because you will need to prove consent years later. Handle every one of those keywords at platform level across all campaign types, immediately rather than at day ten. Enforce frequency caps and quiet hours (8am–9pm local to the recipient) as platform guardrails, not office-toggleable settings. Then layer on CTIA guidelines and 10DLC carrier registration, because carriers will filter unregistered or high-complaint traffic regardless of what the TCPA permits.

PCI DSS — scope reduction is the whole game

Applies to anyone who stores, processes or transmits cardholder data or could affect the security of the cardholder data environment — a broad clause that catches more systems than teams expect. Current version is v4.x.

The design rules that shrink your scope from SAQ D to SAQ A or P2PE: use a hosted payment page or iframe so card data never posts through your server, use P2PE-validated terminals for card-present, store tokens only, never log PAN or CVV, enforce TLS 1.2+. A dental practice is essentially always merchant Level 4; a payment-facilitating software vendor can land much higher.

PCI is contractual, not statutory — enforcement flows through your acquirer and the card networks as fines, higher interchange or loss of processing, not through a regulator. Note that surcharging is governed separately, by card-network rules and a patchwork of state law, which is what PBN’s ZeroPay compliance engine handles.

Glossary

PMS
Practice management system. The system of record: schedule, chart, ledger, claims. Dentrix, Eaglesoft, Open Dental and others.
Operatory
A treatment room, or the chair in it. The scheduling unit in dentistry, the way a room is in a hotel.
DSO
Dental Service Organization. Provides all non-clinical business functions to practices under a management services agreement.
Recall / recare
The standing hygiene cadence, usually six-month. Recare is the modern term.
Reappointment
Booking the next visit before the patient leaves. The highest-leverage retention behaviour in a practice.
Unscheduled treatment
Treatment-planned procedures with no appointment. A concrete, addressable revenue queue.
Case acceptance
Share of diagnosed treatment the patient agrees to. Ask whether it is dollar-based or case-based.
Production / collections
Dentistry performed at full fee, versus cash actually received. Not the same procedures in the same period.
Writeoff
The mandatory contractual difference between a practice’s posted fee and the PPO allowed fee. Distinct from a discretionary adjustment.
UCR fee schedule
Usual, customary and reasonable. The practice’s own posted fee per procedure code, before any payer discount.
CDT
Code on Dental Procedures and Nomenclature. ADA-maintained, HIPAA-mandated, changes every January 1.
270 / 271
The X12 eligibility inquiry and response. The dental 271 is notoriously thin, which is why verification is a product.
837D / 835
Dental claim submission, and the remittance advice that comes back.
EOB / ERA
Explanation of Benefits, human-readable. Electronic Remittance Advice, the machine-readable 835.
Clearinghouse
Sits between practice and payers, normalising formats, scrubbing claims, routing attachments. DentalXChange and Vyne dominate dental.
Attachment
X-rays, perio charts or narratives a payer requires before paying. A dental-specific problem and its own product category.
PPO / FFS / DHMO
Contracted discount for network volume (~82% of policies); no discount, patient pays more; capitated monthly per-member payment.
Annual maximum
The payer’s yearly cap, mostly under $1,500 and unchanged for decades. The reason year-end benefit campaigns work.
Downgrade clause
The payer pays for the cheaper material than the one placed. A frequent source of wrong patient estimates.
Frankenstack
PBN’s term for the six-to-eight disconnected vendor subscriptions a typical practice accumulates. The whole pitch is built on it.
PHI / BAA
Protected health information, and the business associate agreement a vendor must sign to handle it. Appointment metadata alone is PHI.
eConnector
Open Dental’s Windows service on the practice server. The reference example of the on-premise sync agent pattern.
